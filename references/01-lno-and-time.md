# 01 · The LNO framework: where your effort goes

Citations: `[L1 hh:mm:ss]` = Lenny's Podcast 2022 · `[L2 mm:ss]` = Lenny's Podcast live 2024 ·
`[AMP hh:mm:ss]` = Amplitude interview 2021 · `[SS slug]` = his Substack · `[X date]` = his post on X.
Full list in `SOURCES.md`.

> "All your tasks are not created equal." [L1 00:35:02]

## The three kinds of task

| Type | What it returns | In his words |
|---|---|---|
| **L · Leverage** | 10x to 100x | "when you put in a certain amount of effort, you get 10X or 100X in return in terms of impact" [L1 00:35:56] |
| **N · Neutral** | about 1x | "So you put in 1X and you get 1.1 X, those are neutral tasks." [L1 00:35:56] |
| **O · Overhead** | less than 1x | "in terms of impact, you get back a lot less than you actually put in" [L1 00:35:56] |

His one-line summary on X gives the same three bands: Leverage above 10x, Neutral about 1x, Overhead below
1x. [X 2024-04-26](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1783854725999567130)

He credits the origin: "I learned the ideas behind it a while ago via Elizabeth Grace Saunders' INO
Technique & then I modified it to fit product work." [X 2023-03-05](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1632200170799763456)

## The mistake it fixes

> "Many people who are ambitious or are perfectionists like myself by default treat each of these types of
> tasks the same way, and therein lies the problem." [L1 00:35:56]

The fix is matching effort to type: "for the L tasks, let my inner perfectionist shine" [L1 00:36:47], and do
N and O tasks well enough, fast. His own example of an N or O task done fast: a finance request for a long
range projection, which he would usually "get them done in five, 10 minutes", state the assumptions, and ask
how more detail would “materially change” anything. [AMP 01:18:10]

## The same task can be any letter

> "The same type of activity can actually be either an L task or an N task or an O task." [L1 00:37:47]

His example: notes from a meeting can be O, or, when they will shape a contentious decision, "just spend an
hour or even two hours perfecting those notes because it's an L task." [L1 00:39:23]

He made the letter visible: he "prefixed every task in my to-do list with L: or N: or O:" to force himself to
be intentional about effort. [X 2022-02-12](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1492345187665776642)

## Why L tasks don't get done: fear, not time

> "PMs implicitly just deep down they know what their L tasks are." [L1 00:41:43]

> "Because deep down, they don't know if they can formulate a good strategy. So time becomes a convenient
> excuse for us." [L1 00:42:38]

He also uses a deliberate warm-up he calls placebo productivity: for the couple of days before an L task,
"I intentionally do N tasks and O tasks. I fill up my day with N and O tasks." [L1 00:43:46] Then, on the
appointed day, he starts the L task. His advice: "pay attention to your fears because they're telling you something."
[L1 00:45:40] The same point in the Amplitude interview: "What I'm really fearful of when I'm avoiding writing
that PRD that I really should be writing." [AMP 00:30:29]

On beating it, his essay says to recognise that the delay comes "because of some latent fear" and to take the
first step anyway; the fear doesn't go away, but you make more progress with practice.
[SS on-beating-procrastination] His practical trick: "Nothing, for me, at least fights my procrastination for
L tasks better than changing the place from which I'm working." [L1 00:44:26]

## “No time” is usually something else

He lists eight things a “lack of time” often really is in product management: weak prioritization across
teams, lack of focus on what was prioritized, unclear strategy, lack of discipline, lack of courage, missing
staff in key roles, lack of management support, and a misunderstanding of the PM role.
[X 2022-02-14](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1493253710964617216)

And from the live talk: "I am so busy because I'm not making good product decisions." [L2 17:48] Weak features
pile up follow-on work: "you have signed up for even more work for a feature you should not have built in the
first place. That's why we're busy." [L2 23:19]

For leaders, a large part of busyness is "(a) nonsense work you've created for yourself, and (b) work you can
delegate, if you have the courage" [X 2025-05-12](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1921986917945770217). See
`09-leading-product-teams.md` for the delegation half.

## From IC to leader

He calls LNO "career-changing & life-changing for me as a senior IC PM", and names a second tool, the
Radical Delegation Framework, as the equivalent for him as a PM leader.
[X 2023-03-05](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1632200170799763456) Our sources name that framework but do not
explain it, so this repo doesn't either.

## How to apply it (our reading)

The steps, numbers and thresholds below are **our suggestion**, built on his three bands and his fear point.

1. **List the week.** Every task and meeting, one line each.
2. **Letter each one** L, N or O, by asking what it returns in impact, not how hard it is.
3. **Set effort to match.** L gets your best hours and your perfectionism. N gets good enough. O gets the
   minimum, a batch, a delegate, or a no.
4. **Find the avoided L.** The L that has been on the list longest is usually the one you fear. Name the fear
   in one sentence.
5. **Book the first step somewhere else.** A different room or place, per his trick.
6. **Friday check.** Did each L get L effort? Did any O get L effort?

**Worked number (invented):** a PM lead lists 24 items for the week. Three are L (the strategy draft, a
hiring decision, the pre-mortem for a launch), 13 are N, 8 are O. If each O drops from about an hour to 20
minutes, that frees roughly five hours, enough for two long sessions on the strategy draft.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- Labelling everything L, which is the perfectionism he warns about.
- Labelling by activity type (“meetings are O”), which ignores his point that the same activity can be any
  letter.
- Using the O time saved for more N work, so the avoided L still waits.

**Limits:** LNO sorts effort; it doesn't tell you what the right L is. For that you need strategy (04) and
opportunity cost (05).

**Use it now:** `templates/01-lno-week.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. Label every task this week L, N or O. How many Ls are there? (**Our reading:** if it's more than a
   handful, some are probably N.)
2. Which L have you been avoiding? What's the fear behind it?
3. Which N or O tasks are getting L-level polish?
4. If you feel “no time”: which of his eight underlying causes is it?
