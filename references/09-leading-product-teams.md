# 09 · Leading product teams

## The job

His one-post summary of the PM leadership job: the role is to "Make successful products via others & build
self-managing teams", with four responsibilities (strategy and vision, product editing, meta-execution, PM
coaching) and five things to spike on (empathy, listening, influence, product sense, critical and strategic
thinking). [X 2020-07-18](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1284499367383035904)

He says most experienced product leaders can't describe their real role, because nobody taught them; they
repeated what got validation. [AMP 00:18:01] Once you understand it, "What you need to do is to enable
product success through others". [AMP 00:18:01]

> "Actually, as a product leader, you should be spending a ton of time on your desk." [AMP 00:18:01]

If you're in 40 or 50 meetings a week, he says you're not fulfilling the second half of the role, which is
creating self-managing teams. [AMP 00:18:01] And a quieter truth: "a PM leader's job is further lonelier."
[L2 38:18]

On the mess: the turning point for a leader is seeing that the complexity making everything hard "is
actually integral to every leadership job that pays the big bucks".
[X 2025-04-22](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1914492107383038343)

## Self-managing teams: give the why, not the rule

His example of the opposite: a PM tells a designer to make a change only because a senior reviewer has a
quirk about it. "But the why of that is missing." [AMP 00:18:01] What he recommends instead is sharing how
you think about the user goals, the conversion goals, some principles, and the metrics to look at, so the
team can reach the answer itself. [AMP 00:18:01]

## Delegation, and why leaders are busy

He names a Radical Delegation Framework as career-changing for him as a PM leader
[X 2023-03-05](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1632200170799763456), but our sources don't explain it. What they
do say about delegation:

- **Two sources of busyness.** A large part of what makes a PM leader busy is "(a) nonsense work you've created
  for yourself, and (b) work you can delegate, if you have the courage".
  [X 2025-05-12](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1921986917945770217)
- **Nonsense work** includes fires that shouldn't have started or should be left burning, and work that
  looks like positive ROI but has a high opportunity cost (same post).
- **Delegable work** includes many meetings, which can go "to your manager, to someone on your team, or to a
  cross-functional peer" [X 2025-05-12]. He says thinking your case is special is a self-fulfilling prophecy.
- **Assume you won't be on top of everything.** His advice to new managers: "assume there are things you are
  not going to be on top of." Otherwise you will have to attend every meeting. Get the inputs through your
  team, your one-on-ones and team rituals instead. [AMP 01:08:20]
- **Habit and identity** keep leaders stuck: "This is my identity, this is how I operate." Some even compare
  whose calendar is worse. [AMP 01:08:20]

## From IC to manager

He describes himself as "a terrible, terrible manager of PMs" when he first managed, nine months into being a
PM: "I was so sure that I had the answer... And second, I thought my job was to provide the answer."
[AMP 00:11:49] What changed: genuine curiosity, watching his own thoughts, and understanding what a PM manager
is actually for. [AMP 00:11:49]

## Middle management carries the truth

> "The way I view middle management, is they are the organization's way of ensuring that you are able to get
> to the real truth." [AMP 00:16:52]

They sit between the truth on the ground and what executives see at a macro scale, so they must feel free to
say what they see in both directions without risking their jobs. [AMP 00:16:52]

## The meetings he ran at Stripe

From his list of "10 high leverage meetings I swore by"
[X 2023-05-03](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1653611053442555904): a daily end-of-day sync with his engineering
counterpart, a weekly sales sync, 2:2s with the EM and PM of each team, a monthly execution review, a
biweekly business growth meeting, a monthly career chat with his directs (no project talk allowed), product
deep-dives, pre-mortems for big launches, a new team member intro, and birthday celebrations.

## How to apply it (our reading)

1. **Audit a week of meetings.** Mark each one: needed (only you can do it), delegable, or nonsense (shouldn't
   exist).
2. **Delegate two this week** to your manager, a direct report, or a peer, with the why written down.
3. **Replace one status meeting** with an input ritual: a written weekly update or a short execution review.
4. **Block desk time** for strategy and product editing.
5. **Write the why** behind your top three product opinions, so the team can apply them without you.

**Worked number (invented):** a head of product has 38 meetings a week. The audit finds 9 delegable and 6
that shouldn't exist. Removing them frees about 12 hours, which go to two desk blocks for strategy and one
product deep-dive.

**Failure modes (our reading):** delegating the task without the context; cancelling meetings without
replacing the inputs they gave you; insecurity-driven check-ins dressed up as staying close to the details.

**Checks to run:**
1. Who did you last promote or praise publicly? What behaviour did that reward?
2. Which of your interventions this week were insecurity-driven rather than complexity- or taste-based?
3. Which meetings this week could someone else attend?
4. Do your middle managers bring you bad news early?
