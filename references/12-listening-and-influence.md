# 12 · Listening, influence and stakeholders

## Listening is a learnable superpower

> "Listening, *really* listening, is a rare superpower. I was a bad listener most of my life."
> [X 2021-01-17](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1350734022868045825)

He says fixing it made a night and day difference to his leadership (same post). In the Amplitude interview
he describes how [AMP 00:05:13]:

1. **The starting point.** For about 12 years he was “an okay listener”: forming and preparing his response,
   "not really listening to what was said, but hearing what I wanted to hear." [AMP 00:05:13]
2. **A tactic first.** Make sure the other person knows you understood them. He rates that as taking him from
   about four out of ten to six.
3. **Then presence.** "I just want to be a hundred percent present." [AMP 00:05:13] No thinking about the reply, not even
   about how to summarise.
4. **The result.** He came across as more interested, his responses got better, and "The response became a
   question, rather than a statement." [AMP 00:05:13]

His essay compresses the method: "Partition listening and thinking." [SS becoming-great-at-listening] And
listening is a big job: "Your job as a listener is to consume in a few seconds or minutes what the speaker
might have experienced over weeks, months, even decades." [SS becoming-great-at-listening]

## Listening to customers

His customer-conversation habits [PVP 08:10]:
- Start with open questions: how do you do your work, what problems do you hit, what's most frustrating.
- He used to rush through a list of questions and realised he "was merely optimising for quantity rather
  than quality" [PVP 08:10]. Now he goes in with a general objective and follows the customer.
- "Probe further when you hear something unexpected" [PVP 08:10]. On one Stripe project, the team judged each
  conversation by what unexpected thing they heard.
- Being "somewhat clueless about their profession" helps: people share more. [PVP 13:50]

## Nobody can read your mind

His essay calls the belief that others can read your mind the source of most conflict at work, at home, and
in society. [SS 10-ideas-for-2026] One of his nine axioms is simply: "People don't listen very well".
[SS 9-axioms-of-interpersonal-communication]

## Truth has to travel

Middle managers are "the organization's way of ensuring that you are able to get to the real truth", and they
need to feel free to say what they see without fear for their jobs. [AMP 00:16:52] See
`09-leading-product-teams.md`.

## Route around big egos

His tactic when two teams' leaders are clashing: have the negotiation happen between the two teams' engineering managers or tech leads (or at times the
designers) "rather than leading the negotiation yourself." [SS route-around-big-egos] And a
warning about clever resistance: "The smarter the person, the better the excuse." [SS route-around-big-egos]

## Influence and power

Without real influence, he argues, product teams "end up shipping lower quality and less innovative products
over time." [SS influence-power-and-product-management]

## Blunt feedback: when to take it

> "Blunt feedback can be life-changing, but only if it comes from someone with: a) the right intent, and b)
> astute judgment in the specific subject matter" [SS on-blunt-feedback]

Both fail more often than people admit, he says. Bad intent can be subtle: the real audience is the
backchannel, where the giver later attaches a label to you. Good intent with weak judgment pattern-matches
from a lower skill level. He adds that for most people this is bad advice, since they should stay open to
feedback; it applies to people at higher levels of proficiency. [SS on-blunt-feedback]

## The Genuine Anti-Sell

> "If you are very competent, the Genuine Anti-Sell works like magic." [SS the-genuine-anti-sell]

**What it is (paraphrase of his essay):** be candid about why you might not be a good fit, for example when
interviewing for a senior leadership role. A competent hiring manager is surprised by your awareness, you
stand out from candidates who are selling, and if they already rate you, they argue back with reasons you
fit, which strengthens their belief. He says it works in other influence situations too (hiring, convincing a
group, selling a product), but only for very few people, only if you are competent, and only if it is
genuine: "This is not a manipulation technique." If you use it as one, it will backfire. [SS the-genuine-anti-sell]

## How to apply it (our reading)

**In your next three 1:1s:**
1. Don't prepare a reply while they talk.
2. After they finish, ask one question that only makes sense if you heard them.
3. Write down one thing you heard that you didn't expect.

**In a stakeholder conflict:**
1. Write what each side believes the other knows. Most gaps are here.
2. If two leaders are dug in, set up the negotiation one level down, between their leads.
3. Before acting on blunt feedback, check the giver's intent and their judgment in that area.

**Worked scenario (invented):** a head of product and a head of sales argue every week about a custom
feature for one large prospect. A listening 1:1 reveals sales believes product has data showing the segment
is unimportant; product has none. The question moves from who wins to what we would need to learn, and
the two teams' leads set up three customer calls to find out.

**Failure modes (our reading):** performative listening (nodding, then the prepared answer); routing around
egos so often that leaders are never in the room; using the anti-sell as a tactic, which he says will backfire.

**Checks to run:**
1. In your last 1:1, did you separate listening from preparing your reply?
2. Which conflict right now comes from assuming the other side knows what you know?
3. Is there a negotiation you're leading that should happen one level down?
