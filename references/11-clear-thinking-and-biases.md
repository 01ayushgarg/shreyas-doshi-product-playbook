# 11 · Clear thinking and the biases that cloud it

> "It is about the quality of your thinking." [SS sometimes-you-should-talk-to-fewer]

## Explain by analogy, don't decide by it

> "You should explain by an evocative analogy (to help people understand your decisions) but you should not
> reason by an evocative analogy (to arrive at your decisions)" [SS the-antithesis-principle]

> "A clever analogy is never a good reason for why you should do something." [SS 10-ideas-for-2026]

The same essay warns that the appeal of feeling smart can beat the goal of making the correct decision.
[SS 10-ideas-for-2026]

## Biases he names

| Bias | What it is |
|---|---|
| Authority | Accepting an idea "because Bezos said it" [L2 33:53] |
| Alliteration | "we get very impressed with alliterations" [L2 34:58] |
| “I already know that” | He calls it "the greatest invisible barrier" to learning [SS 10-ideas-for-2026] |
| Apple pie positions | A statement that elevates the speaker and is hard to push back on, so "everyone avoids the personal risk" and nods [X 2023-02-23](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1628567045800591361) |
| Blaming distribution | Believing the product is great and the problem is marketing and distribution [SS the-fundamental-cognitive-bias-of] |
| Execution orientation fallacy | Choosing something because it's easy to execute, for "the satisfaction of getting started" [AMP 00:54:37] |
| Analytical over creative | Favouring charts and tables, and discounting “product creative” and "instinct and judgment" [AMP 00:44:14] |

The fix for the first two: "evaluate the idea separate from all of its social proof, and authority proof."
[L2 36:41]

If you only take two biases seriously, he names them: "As long as you firmly believe two: confirmation bias
and fundamental attribution bias." [AMP 00:54:37]

## Talk about biases as a team, not about a person

> "This is why I don't talk about individual cognitive biases. I usually talk about our cognitive biases at
> the level of teams." [AMP 00:54:37]

It is hard to tell a colleague they are falling for a bias without it sounding personal. So he names, up
front, the biases the group is likely to fall for, sets the expectation that people will flag them, and says
flagging is “not a weakness”. He puts the onus on the leader. [AMP 00:54:37] This is the same idea as the
pre-mortem's shared vocabulary (`02-pre-mortems.md`).

## Make room for creative intelligence

In most product meetings, he says, you look more competent showing several charts and a couple of tables,
and the recommendations end up “in this box”. If creative intelligence were allowed, conclusions would
"contain some wacky ideas", things that don't strictly follow from the data. People who bring those get odd
looks, then feedback to "back things up better". [AMP 00:44:14]

## Think, then write (or not)

He argues many people over-rely on writing because "for many that's the only time they are doing clear
thinking", and that it is "quite smart to also develop the habit of thinking clearly without writing",
because "there is an opportunity cost to writing." [SS on-the-confusion-between-writing] With clarity of
thought, writing without AI "takes way less time than making AI write it."
[X 2026-09-29](https://x.com/shreyas/status/2104967583158149378)

## How to apply it (our reading)

1. **Before a big decision,** write the main reason in one sentence. If it's an analogy, or the fact that some famous
   company does it, find the real reason or stop.
2. **Strip the author.** Re-read the proposal as if a new hire wrote it. Does it still hold?
3. **Name the team's likely biases** at the start of a project (`templates/10-bias-checklist.md`) and agree
   that flagging them is expected.
4. **Ask for one wacky option** in every review, labelled as such, so creative ideas get a slot.
5. **Check the two** he insists on: are we only seeing evidence that confirms us, and are we blaming people
   for what the situation caused?

**Worked scenario (invented):** a team wants to copy a famous company's team structure. The one-sentence
reason is the analogy itself. Stripping it, the real problem is slow decisions between two teams, which the
diagnosis in chapter 04 traces to a missing strategy choice. Reorganising wouldn't have fixed it.

**Failure modes (our reading):** using the bias list to win arguments; flagging biases in others but never
in yourself; replacing analysis with “wacky ideas” rather than adding them.

**Use it now:** `templates/10-bias-checklist.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. In the last big decision, was an analogy doing the reasoning?
2. Which recent proposal was accepted because of who said it?
3. Which statements in your last review were apple pie positions?
