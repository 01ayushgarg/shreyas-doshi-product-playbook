# 05 · Opportunity cost and prioritization

## Stop maximising ROI

> "When you are in a high leverage role, you should stop doing work that simply provides a positive return on
> investment, ROI. And you should start focusing on work that minimizes opportunity cost." [L1 01:02:47]

Why ROI misleads: "what we end up doing to get high ROI on our work is we end up trying to decrease the
denominator" [L1 01:03:48]. "The problem is we just fill up our plate with quick wins." [L1 01:04:36]

> "Opportunity cost is simply the value of the optimal option minus the value of the chosen option."
> [L1 01:04:36]

The question that replaces ROI is whether this is the best use of your time [L1 01:05:27], or in his newer
phrasing: "Is this the very best use of my time & energy?" [SS sometimes-you-should-talk-to-fewer]

Don't over-engineer it: "I do not recommend trying to quantify opportunity costs." [L1 01:08:51]

## Allocate the portfolio

> "I would like us to target about 60% of our time on incrementals." [L1 01:09:45]

About 30% goes to bigger new bets: "And because it's 30%, it can't be five big new initiatives, it's probably
one or two." [L1 01:10:43] The remaining 10% goes to stability and infrastructure. [L1 01:10:43] His caveat
on the numbers: "pick whatever is right for you." [L1 01:09:45]

## Small teams on fast-growing products

He endorses Derek Sivers' line "If it isn't a HELL YEAH! it is a No." for product prioritization, and adds
that on a fast-growing product with a tiny team, "this is likely the only prioritization" framework you
need. [X 2021-09-06](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1434940492685266948)

## Prioritization beats parallelization

> "You do not rise to the level of your parallelization. You fall to the level of your prioritization."
> [X 2021-04-08](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1380197002265251843)

He describes a large part of the PM manager's job as good prioritization, plus explaining the rationale so
others understand it. [X 2023-03-11](https://x.com/shreyas/status/1634428355616591875)

## Best practices raise the floor, not the ceiling

> "Corporate best practices are designed to raise the floor, not the ceiling." [SS sometimes-you-should-talk-to-fewer]

## How to apply it (our reading)

The steps and thresholds here are **our suggestion**, anchored to his opportunity-cost definition and the
60/30/10 guide.

1. **List the plan.** Every project for the quarter, with rough effort.
2. **Name the best alternative** for each project: the thing you'd do with that time instead.
3. **Ask his question per row:** is this the very best use of our time? Don't compute a number.
4. **Sort into buckets** (incremental, big new initiative, stability) and compare with his rough guide.
5. **Cut the cheap quick wins** that are there mainly because they're cheap.

**Worked number (invented):** a team of 6 engineers has about 70 engineer-weeks in a quarter. The plan has
11 projects: 8 small features (40 weeks), 3 big initiatives (25 weeks) and almost nothing for reliability.
That's about 57% / 36% / 7%. Close to his guide on paper, but three big bets for this team means none gets
full attention. **Our suggestion:** drop one big bet, fold its 8 weeks into the other two, and move 2 weeks
to reliability, giving about 57% / 33% / 10%.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- Treating 60/30/10 as a rule. He gives it as a target and tells you to pick what's right for you.
- Running “big initiatives” in parallel. **Our reading:** his “probably one or two” means that more than two
  at once is a warning sign for most teams.
- Quantifying opportunity cost in a spreadsheet, which he advises against.

**Use it now:** `templates/05-opportunity-cost-review.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. List your team's top 5 projects. For each: what's the best alternative you're not doing?
2. What share is incremental, big new initiatives, stability and infrastructure? Is it near his 60/30/10?
3. How many big new initiatives are running? (He says “probably one or two”.)
4. Which item is a quick win chosen mainly because it's cheap?
